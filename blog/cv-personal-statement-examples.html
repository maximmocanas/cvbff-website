CV Personal Statement Examples (That Actually Work)

May 2026 · 7 min read

The personal statement, also called a CV summary or professional profile, sits at the very top of your CV and is read before anything else. It should answer one question in three or four sentences: why is this person right for this specific role?

Most personal statements fail to answer that question. They're full of empty phrases that apply to every candidate and every job, which means they waste the only seconds a recruiter gives to the top of your CV before scanning down.

This guide covers what makes a personal statement work, a formula you can apply to any role, and real examples across different industries and career stages.

What a Bad Personal Statement Looks Like

You've seen these. You've probably written one. They look like this:

Generic, don't do this

"A highly motivated and results-driven professional with a passion for excellence and a proven track record of success. I am a dedicated team player who thrives in fast-paced environments and is committed to delivering high-quality outcomes. I am now looking for a challenging new opportunity where I can utilise my skills and experience to contribute to a dynamic organisation."

Every word of that could have been written by any candidate for any job. It tells the recruiter nothing about who you are, what you've done, or why you fit this role. It reads like a template, because it is one.

The other common failure is the biography: a chronological summary of your career that duplicates what's already in the work experience section. A personal statement is not a recap. It's a pitch.

The Formula That Works

A strong personal statement has four components, in roughly this order:

[Job title/area] with [X years of experience] in [relevant context, sector, type of company, specialisation]. Known for [2–3 specific strengths that match this role]. Most recently [one concrete result or achievement]. Now seeking [what you want, optional, brief].

You don't need to follow this rigidly, and you don't need all four parts in every statement. But hitting at least the first three produces a statement that's specific enough to be useful.

Crucially: rewrite it for every application. Mirror the language in the job posting. If the posting talks about "stakeholder engagement", your statement should too. If it emphasises "data-driven decision making", that phrase should appear. The personal statement is the easiest part of your CV to tailor and the part recruiters read most carefully.

Examples by Role and Career Stage

Marketing Manager

✓ Strong personal statement

"Content marketing manager with 6 years of experience driving organic growth for B2B SaaS companies. Specialise in SEO strategy, editorial planning, and cross-functional collaboration with sales and product. Grew organic traffic from 12K to 85K monthly sessions at my last company through a structured content programme. Looking for a senior IC or team lead role where content is treated as a strategic growth channel."

This works because it's specific: B2B SaaS is a context, not a genre. "SEO strategy and editorial planning" are real skills, not soft descriptors. The traffic number is concrete and credible. The final sentence signals seniority without being vague.

Software Engineer

✓ Strong personal statement

"Full-stack engineer with 4 years building production React and Node.js applications in fintech and e-commerce. Comfortable across the stack, from API design and database optimisation to component architecture and CI/CD pipelines. Most recently reduced page load time by 60% on a high-traffic checkout flow, improving conversion by 12%. Interested in roles where engineering quality and product thinking overlap."

Notice how the tech stack is named specifically. "React and Node.js" is indexable by ATS systems. The result, load time and conversion improvement, is the kind of impact an engineering hiring manager cares about. "Engineering quality and product thinking" signals culture fit without being hollow.

Project Manager

✓ Strong personal statement

"Certified project manager (PMP) with 8 years delivering complex programmes across financial services and professional services. Experienced managing cross-functional teams of up to 20 people and budgets up to £4M. Track record of recovering at-risk projects, 3 of the last 4 programmes delivered under budget and ahead of schedule. Looking to move into a portfolio management role within a regulated industry."

Certifications go in the first sentence because they're a filter criterion. The budget and team size give the recruiter a calibration for seniority. The "recovering at-risk projects" line is memorable and differentiating, most PMs don't volunteer that they've had to rescue things, which is precisely what makes it credible.

Recent Graduate

✓ Strong personal statement

"Recent Economics graduate (2:1, University of Edinburgh) with hands-on experience in data analysis and financial modelling through two internships in investment operations. Proficient in Python, Excel, and SQL, applied to real client datasets during a placement at an asset management firm. Looking for an analyst role where I can develop technical skills in a structured environment while contributing to commercial outcomes."

For graduates, the degree, university, and classification belong in the statement because they're often used as initial filters. The internship experience is framed in terms of what was actually done, not just where. The technical skills are named specifically so ATS systems pick them up.

Career Changer

✓ Strong personal statement

"Former secondary school teacher transitioning into instructional design and corporate L&D. Spent 7 years designing and delivering curriculum for 250+ students, with a measurable impact on exam outcomes (departmental pass rate from 61% to 78% over 3 years). Now completing a CIPD Level 5 L&D qualification and seeking a role where education design skills transfer directly to workplace learning challenges."

Career changers need to do two things at once: acknowledge the pivot and immediately translate past experience into relevant value. This example does both. The exam outcome result proves impact, not just intent. The in-progress qualification signals commitment, not dabbling.

What to Avoid in Every Statement

  • "Passionate about", passion is expected; it's not a distinguishing trait
  • "Strong communication skills", every candidate claims this; demonstrate it through the quality of your writing instead
  • "Team player", vague, unverifiable, and on every CV in the pile
  • "Looking for a challenging role", what role isn't? This adds nothing
  • Referring to yourself in third person, "John is a results-driven professional" reads oddly in a personal statement
  • Going over 5 sentences, beyond that you're writing an essay, not a pitch

Length and Format

Three to four sentences is the sweet spot. Some roles (particularly senior or creative positions) support five. Never more than that, the personal statement is the appetiser, not the main course. Its job is to make the recruiter want to read the rest of your CV, not to replace it.

Don't use bullet points in your personal statement. It should read as a cohesive paragraph, bullets fragment it and undermine the sense that you're presenting a coherent case for yourself.

The Most Important Rule

Write your personal statement last, after you've reviewed the job posting carefully and updated the rest of your CV. By then you know exactly which three or four things about your background are most relevant to this role. Lead with those.

A personal statement written before you've read the job posting is almost always too generic. Write it last. Make it a response to the posting, not a summary of your career.

If you're applying to ten similar roles, you might have five or six variations of your statement, each emphasising slightly different strengths depending on what each company cares about most. That's not over-engineering; that's basic targeting.
