CV vs Resume: What's the Difference and Which Do You Need?

May 2026 · 5 min read

If you've searched for job application advice online, you've probably seen both terms used, sometimes interchangeably, sometimes as if they're entirely different documents. The confusion is understandable, because the answer genuinely depends on where you are in the world and what kind of role you're applying for.

Here's the definitive breakdown.

The Short Answer

Quick reference

  • UK, Europe, Australia, most of the world: Say "CV". It means a 1–2 page document covering your work history, education, and skills. Resume means the same thing here, both terms are acceptable and widely used.
  • US and Canada (corporate/commercial jobs): Say "resume". It means a concise 1-page document tailored to the specific job. "CV" is rarely used in this context.
  • US and Canada (academic, research, medical): Say "CV". It means a comprehensive multi-page document covering your full academic career, publications, presentations, and credentials.

The safest rule: use whatever term the job posting uses. If the application says "upload your CV", submit a CV. If it says "resume", submit a resume. In practice, the document itself is usually the same, it's the terminology that varies by geography.

A Closer Look: CV vs Resume by Country

The Real Difference: US Academic CV vs Everyone Else

The biggest actual difference in content, not just terminology, is the US academic CV. This is where the confusion originates.

In the US, when applying for a university faculty position, research fellowship, or senior medical role, you submit a CV that is nothing like a standard job application document. It includes:

  • Complete list of publications (peer-reviewed papers, books, book chapters)
  • Conference presentations and invited talks
  • Research grants awarded
  • Teaching history
  • Professional service (journal reviewing, committee memberships)
  • Awards and honours

A senior academic's CV might be 15–25 pages long. It is a comprehensive record of an academic career, not a targeted pitch for a single role. This is completely different from a CV as used in the UK, and it explains why the two terms genuinely describe different documents, in the US academic context.

For everyone else, applying for commercial, corporate, or non-academic roles anywhere in the world, the distinction is purely terminological. The document is the same: 1–2 pages, recent work history, education, skills, targeted to the role.

Does the Length Actually Differ?

In the UK and US commercial context: a resume tends to be held to a stricter 1-page standard, while a UK CV is typically allowed to be 2 pages for experienced professionals. But this is convention, not a hard rule, and it's shifting.

The practical guidance is the same regardless of what you call it:

  • Under 5 years of experience: aim for 1 page
  • 5–10 years of experience: 1–2 pages
  • 10+ years: up to 2 pages; a third page only if every line earns its place

What About the Content, Does That Differ?

In the US, it's standard to omit certain personal details that would appear on a UK CV: no date of birth, no photo, no marital status. This is partly legal (US employers want to avoid discrimination claims tied to age, appearance, or family status) and partly convention.

In the UK, photos are optional (and increasingly rare in modern CVs). Date of birth is technically optional but still common. Nationality or right to work status is sometimes included.

For an international job search, the cleanest approach is to omit personal details beyond contact information and LinkedIn URL. It's safe in every market and avoids any inadvertent disadvantage.

The Bottom Line

For the vast majority of job seekers applying for commercial roles anywhere in the world: CV and resume describe the same document. Write a strong, tailored 1–2 page document covering your work history, education, and skills, then call it whatever the employer calls it.

The terminology is a regional convention. The quality of the document is what actually determines whether you get an interview.

The more important question isn't what to call your document, it's whether it's tailored to the specific role you're applying for. A generic CV by any name is less effective than a targeted one.
