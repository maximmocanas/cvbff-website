How to Write a CV With No Experience (Student & Graduate Guide 2026)

May 2026 · 9 min read

Writing your first CV is intimidating in a specific way: every guide you read assumes you've already had a job. So you sit there staring at a blank page, wondering how you're supposed to fill it with "experience" when you've never been hired.

Good news: you have more to work with than you think. The problem isn't a lack of material, it's that you've been taught to think of "experience" too narrowly. Coursework, projects, volunteering, part-time jobs, sports, society roles, side projects, hackathons, these all count when framed right. This guide shows you how to build a CV that gets interviews when you've never been paid for a real job.

What "Experience" Actually Means on a CV

Employers hiring entry-level candidates know you don't have a track record. What they're actually looking for is evidence that you can do the work. That evidence can come from anywhere.

Things that count as evidence of capability:

  • University coursework, especially group projects, dissertations, lab work, case studies
  • Personal projects, a website you built, an analysis you wrote up, a podcast you produced
  • Volunteering, organising a charity event, running a society's social media
  • Part-time or summer jobs, even retail and hospitality teach customer service, time management, and dealing with pressure
  • Sports and society leadership, captain of a team, treasurer of a society, organiser of an event
  • Online courses and certifications, especially ones that produce tangible output (a portfolio piece, a Kaggle notebook, a Coursera capstone)
  • Hackathons and competitions, even if you didn't win

If you can describe what you did, what challenge it solved, and what the outcome was, it belongs on your CV.

The Structure: 5 Sections, In This Order

For a no-experience CV, the order matters more than usual. You want the strongest material at the top, not buried at the bottom.

  1. Header, name, email, phone, LinkedIn, location (city only, no full address)
  2. Personal statement, 3 to 4 lines that frame who you are and what you're looking for
  3. Education, comes first because it's your strongest credential right now
  4. Projects, internships or relevant experience, your evidence of capability
  5. Skills, interests and additional info, keep tight, no padding

One page. Always. Recruiters spend 10 to 15 seconds on entry-level CVs; two pages cuts your attention in half.

Writing the Personal Statement With No Experience

The personal statement at the top is your one chance to frame yourself before the recruiter reads anything else. Don't waste it on filler.

A weak example:

Recent graduate with a degree in Business Management, looking for a challenging role in a dynamic environment where I can grow my skills.

What's wrong: every entry-level candidate could write this. It says nothing about you.

A better example:

Business graduate from the University of Manchester with a focus on consumer behaviour and data analysis. Built a market-research project on Gen Z banking habits that was used as a case study by the lecturer. Looking for a graduate role in product or strategy at a consumer brand.

What's different: it's specific. It tells the reader what you actually studied, what you produced, and what you want next. It also makes the rest of the CV feel coherent because it sets up what to look for.

Education: Lead With What Matters

Don't just list your degree and grade. Add the specifics that show capability:

  • Relevant modules, pick three or four most relevant to the job, list them by name
  • Dissertation or final project, title, one line on what you did, one line on the outcome
  • Awards or scholarships, including small ones like dean's list or top mark in a module
  • Group project highlights, anything where you led, presented, or delivered something tangible

BSc Computer Science, 2:1, University of Edinburgh, 2023–2026

Final project: Built a real-time bus tracking web app for Edinburgh, used by 300+ students within a month of launch. Wrote up in dissertation that received a first.

Relevant modules: Software Engineering, Database Systems, Human-Computer Interaction, Machine Learning.

The "Experience" Section When You Don't Have Job Experience

Rename it. Call it "Projects", "Relevant Experience", or "Experience & Projects". Mix any paid work, internships, and substantial projects into one section, in reverse chronological order.

For each entry, use this format:

  • Role / project name, Organisation or context, Dates
  • One sentence on the context
  • Two or three bullet points: what you did, what tools, what outcome

Here's a good example for a side project:

Personal Project: Spotify Listening Habits Analysis, Independent, Spring 2026

Analysed 4 years of personal Spotify data using Python and Streamlit to identify listening patterns by time of day and mood.

  • Pulled and cleaned 50,000+ play records using the Spotify API
  • Built an interactive dashboard with 6 visualisations
  • Wrote up findings in a Medium article that hit 2,000 views

This is "experience" worth more than a generic shop assistant role, because it shows initiative, technical skill, and the ability to ship something.

Don't Underestimate Part-Time and Customer-Facing Jobs

Many students undersell their summer jobs. "Worked at Costa" sounds unimpressive until you reframe it:

Barista, Costa Coffee, Manchester, Summers 2024 and 2025

  • Handled 200+ customer interactions per shift in a high-volume city centre branch
  • Trained two new staff members on espresso machine operation and till procedures
  • Recognised as employee of the month in August 2024 for reducing average serving time by 15%

Same job, totally different impression. The work didn't change, the framing did.

The Skills Section: What Actually Belongs

Skip the generic stuff. "Microsoft Office", "team player", "problem solver" are noise. Recruiters glaze past these because every CV has them.

What to include:

  • Specific tools and software, Python, R, Figma, Adobe Creative Suite, SQL, Excel (specify advanced functions like VLOOKUP, pivots, macros)
  • Languages, with level (e.g. "French, Conversational", not just "French")
  • Methodologies or frameworks if relevant, Agile, Lean Startup, Design Thinking
  • Certifications, Google Analytics, AWS Cloud Practitioner, Coursera specialisations

The Mistakes Almost Every First-Time CV Makes

  • Two pages. No. One page. If you can't fit it, cut.
  • A photo. Don't, unless you're applying outside the UK or to a role where appearance matters (acting, modelling). It triggers unconscious bias filters in screening.
  • Listing every GCSE. "9 GCSEs A*–C including Maths and English" is enough.
  • Generic interests section. "Reading, travelling, socialising with friends" tells a recruiter nothing. Either be specific ("Long-form non-fiction, currently working through Tooze's history of finance") or skip it.
  • Vague claims. "Strong communication skills" is a claim. "Presented final-year project to 80-person audience and won best presentation" is evidence.
  • Sending the same CV to every job. Even at entry level, employers can tell. Tailor it for each role, even if the changes are small.

A Realistic Outcome

Even a perfect first CV won't get you a response to every application. The graduate market is competitive and a lot of the outcome depends on factors outside your CV: timing, role fit, internal referrals, the company's hiring freeze that nobody told you about.

What a good CV does is maximise your hit rate for the applications you do send. With a well-structured CV, tailored for each role, you should expect a response (positive or negative) on 15 to 25% of applications. Without tailoring, that drops to under 5%.
