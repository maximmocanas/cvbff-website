How to Beat ATS Systems in 2026 (Without Gaming the System)

May 2026 · 8 min read

Applicant Tracking Systems, ATS, are the software that most mid-to-large employers use to manage job applications. Your CV almost certainly passes through one before a human recruiter ever sees it. And depending on how your CV is structured and written, it may not make it through at all.

The good news: you don't need to trick or "game" an ATS to pass it. The same things that make a CV readable to a human, clear structure, relevant language, specific skills, also make it parseable by an ATS. The goal is alignment, not manipulation.

How ATS Systems Actually Work

There's a lot of myth around ATS. The most persistent one is that they read your CV like a human and score it on some invisible rubric. The reality is more specific and, once you understand it, more manageable.

Most ATS platforms do a few things:

  • Parse your CV into structured data, they extract your name, contact info, work history, education, and skills into fields they can filter and search. If your formatting breaks the parser, this data is lost.
  • Match against job requirements, recruiters set up filters for the role (required skills, minimum years of experience, qualifications). CVs that don't match are automatically ranked lower or filtered out.
  • Keyword score your CV, some systems score how many of the job description's key terms appear in your CV, and in which sections. Higher scores rank higher in the recruiter's queue.

The most widely used ATS platforms, Greenhouse, Lever, Workday, iCIMS, Taleo, all work broadly this way, though the specifics vary. The strategies below work across all of them.

The Formatting Rules That Actually Matter

ATS parsers are better than they used to be, but they still struggle with certain formatting choices. These are the ones that cause real problems:

Avoid tables, text boxes, and columns

Many people use two-column layouts or tables to make their CV look more structured. ATS parsers often read these left-to-right across the full width, merging content from different columns into nonsense. A single-column layout is always safe.

Avoid headers and footers

Contact information in the header or footer of a Word document is frequently missed by ATS parsers. Put your name, email, phone, and LinkedIn URL in the main body of the document, not in the document's header section.

Use standard section headings

Creative heading names like "Where I've Been" or "What I'm Good At" confuse parsers trained on standard terminology. Stick to: Work Experience, Education, Skills, Certifications, Summary. These are what parsers are built to recognise.

Submit as PDF or Word, but know the difference

PDF is generally safe for modern ATS systems (post-2020). Older systems parse Word documents more reliably. If the job posting specifies a format, use it. If it doesn't, PDF is the better default, it preserves your formatting exactly and is readable by all modern ATS.

Don't use images, icons, or graphics

A skills bar chart, a photo, or decorative icons might look good to a human reader, but ATS systems can't read them. Any information conveyed through visuals is invisible to the parser.

Quick test

Try copying the text from your CV and pasting it into a plain text editor (like Notepad). If the information comes out in a logical order and nothing important is missing, your CV is likely ATS-parseable. If it's a jumbled mess, your formatting is probably causing problems.

The Keyword Strategy That Works

This is where most people lose points they should be winning. ATS keyword matching is straightforward once you know how it works, and there's a legitimate, honest way to optimise for it.

Use the exact language from the job posting

ATS systems often match keywords exactly or near-exactly. "Stakeholder management" and "stakeholder engagement" might not match. "Project management" and "programme management" are different terms. "SQL" and "Structured Query Language" may or may not be treated as equivalent depending on the system.

The rule: if you genuinely have the skill being asked for, use the same words they use to describe it. Don't paraphrase.

Read the job posting as a keyword list. Every skill, tool, qualification, and competency named is a potential matching term. If you have it, make sure your CV says it, in their words, not yours.

Cover the most important keywords in multiple sections

Some ATS systems weight keyword frequency and location. A skill mentioned in your summary, your skills section, and in a work experience bullet point is more likely to score than one mentioned only in passing. For your most critical skills, the ones listed first in the job requirements, make sure they appear more than once.

Don't keyword stuff

There's a version of "ATS optimisation" advice online that tells you to paste the job description into your CV in white text, or cram keywords into your skills section whether you have those skills or not. Don't do this. Modern ATS systems flag keyword stuffing. And if your CV somehow gets through, a human reads it next, and they'll see immediately that the claims don't hold up. You'll waste your time and theirs.

Your Skills Section: the ATS Quick Win

The skills section is the easiest part of your CV to tailor for ATS, and the most often neglected. Most people write it once and never touch it again.

For each job you apply for:

  • Pull out every specific tool, technology, methodology, and competency named in the job posting
  • Cross-reference with your skills section, do you actually have those skills?
  • Add any missing skills you genuinely have (using the exact terminology from the posting)
  • Move the most relevant skills to the top of the section, don't make the parser hunt
  • Remove skills that are irrelevant to this role, they dilute the signal

This takes 5 minutes and consistently moves CVs up the ATS ranking.

What ATS Can't Filter (and Why That Matters)

ATS systems filter on keywords, structure, and explicit criteria like years of experience and qualifications. They can't evaluate the quality of your writing, the credibility of your achievements, or the coherence of your career story. That's still the human's job.

This is important because it means passing ATS is necessary but not sufficient. A keyword-optimised CV with weak bullet points will pass the filter and still fail to get you an interview. The goal is a CV that passes ATS and compels a human to act on it.

The good news: the same work that makes your CV compelling to a human, specific, quantified achievements; clear progression; language that mirrors the job posting, also improves ATS performance. They're not in tension. Optimise for both at once.

ATS Optimisation Checklist

  • Single-column layout with no tables or text boxes
  • Contact details in the main body, not in a document header
  • Standard section headings (Work Experience, Education, Skills)
  • Submitted as PDF (or Word if specified)
  • No images, icons, or graphics carrying important information
  • Keywords from the job posting used verbatim throughout the CV
  • Most important skills appear in summary, skills section, and experience bullets
  • Skills section updated for this specific role
  • Passes the plain-text paste test

The Things That Don't Help

  • White text keyword stuffing, flagged by modern ATS and immediately disqualifying if a human sees it
  • Paying for "ATS-certified" CV templates, no such certification exists
  • Including every keyword from the posting regardless of whether you have the skill, this is lying and it shows in interviews
  • Worrying about font choice, ATS doesn't care about your typography

The underlying truth about ATS is that it rewards the same CV that would impress a good recruiter anyway: one that's clearly structured, uses the language of the role, and makes a specific case for why this candidate is right for this job. The optimisation and the quality are the same work.
