How to Write a Cover Letter That Gets Read (2026 Examples)

May 2026 · 8 min read

Here's the uncomfortable truth: most cover letters never get read past the first sentence. Recruiters scanning hundreds of applications spend 10 to 15 seconds on each one, and if the opening line is a generic "I am writing to apply for the X role at Y company", they're already on the next CV.

But cover letters aren't dead. A good one still moves your application from the "maybe" pile to the "yes" pile, especially for roles where multiple candidates have similar CVs. The trick is knowing what makes a cover letter actually worth reading in 2026, which is very different from what people taught you in school.

The 4-Part Structure That Actually Works

Forget the five-paragraph essay format. A modern cover letter has four parts, fits on half a page, and reads more like a confident email than a formal letter.

  1. A specific opening that shows you understand the role and the company, not a template hello.
  2. One paragraph of relevant proof, ideally a concrete result or project tied to what they need.
  3. One paragraph of context, why this role and why this company, briefly.
  4. A simple close, no grovelling, no fake humility, no "I look forward to hearing from you at your earliest convenience".

That's it. Around 200 to 300 words total. If you're writing more than that, you're padding.

The Opening: Three Templates That Beat "I Am Writing To Apply"

The opening line is the single highest-leverage sentence in your application. It either earns you another 30 seconds of reading time or it doesn't. Here are three opening patterns that work in 2026.

1. Open with a specific observation about the company

Your recent move to a usage-based pricing model is one of the smartest pivots I've seen a B2B SaaS company make this year, and it's exactly the kind of product strategy I'd want to work on next.

This works because it proves you've actually researched them. Most applicants haven't. Just doing this puts you in the top 10%.

2. Open with a sharp credential tied to their need

I've spent the last three years owning paid acquisition for a Series B fintech, scaling spend from £200K to £3M a month while keeping CAC payback under nine months. The growth marketing role at Monzo sounds like exactly the kind of next challenge I'm looking for.

This works when your experience is directly relevant. Lead with the strongest, most specific thing you've done.

3. Open with a brief, honest hook

I've been a customer of yours for three years. When I saw you were hiring a content lead, I closed my laptop and started writing this. Here's why I think I'd be a great fit.

This works when there's a genuine story behind your interest. Don't fake it, but if there's a real connection, lead with it.

The Middle: Connect Your Experience to Their Role, Specifically

The middle of a cover letter is where most people lose the reader. They list their experience as if reading from their CV, which the recruiter has already read. The middle should do something the CV can't do: explicitly connect what you've done to what they need.

Pick two or three things from the job description that you have strong evidence for, and address them directly. Don't try to cover everything. Three well-chosen proof points beat a wall of generic claims.

The cover letter exists to do what your CV can't: explain why your specific experience matches their specific need.

If they want someone who can "build a team from scratch", say "I built a team of five from zero in 18 months at my last company". If they want "cross-functional leadership", say "I led the launch of X with engineering, design, and sales as joint leads". Mirror their language, prove it with a result.

The "Why This Role" Paragraph: Brief and Honest

Hiring managers want to know you actually want this job, not just any job. But they don't want a love letter. Two or three sentences are enough.

Avoid:

  • "I've always been passionate about..." (nobody believes this)
  • "Your company is a leader in..." (you're describing them back to themselves)
  • "This role is a great fit for my skills" (every applicant says this)

Instead, give a specific reason: a problem you'd want to work on, a part of their product or mission that resonates, a stage of growth that matches what you're looking for next.

The Close: Don't Apologise for Yourself

End with confidence, not deference. You're applying because you believe you'd be valuable to them, not because you're hoping they'll take pity.

A clean close looks like:

Happy to walk through any of this in more detail. My CV is attached, and I'd be glad to chat whenever works for you.

That's all you need. No "I look forward to hearing from you at your earliest convenience". No "Thank you so much for considering my application". Just polite, direct, done.

Format and Length

  • Length: 200 to 300 words. If it doesn't fit on half a page, cut it.
  • Format: Plain text inside the email body or as a one-page PDF. Skip the formal letterhead with addresses, the recruiter doesn't need your home address.
  • Salutation: Address a real person if you can find one ("Hi Sarah"). If not, "Hi team" is fine. Avoid "To whom it may concern" and "Dear Sir/Madam".
  • Tone: Match the company. A startup expects something more direct; a law firm or a bank expects something more formal. Read their careers page for cues.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Reusing the same letter for every job. Recruiters can spot a template instantly. A tailored opening line and one tailored proof point is the minimum.
  • Repeating your CV. The cover letter should add context the CV can't, not summarise it.
  • Excessive formality. "I am writing to express my keen interest" sounds dated. Write like a confident human.
  • Long blocks of text. Use short paragraphs. Use white space. Recruiters skim.
  • Mentioning weaknesses. Don't apologise for what you lack. Focus on what you have.
