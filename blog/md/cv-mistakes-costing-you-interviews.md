# 5 Resume Mistakes That Are Costing You Interviews

> Not getting interviews? These 5 common resume and CV mistakes are probably why — and all of them are quick fixes once you know what to look for.

If you've been applying to jobs and hearing nothing back, your CV is almost certainly the problem. Not because your experience isn't good enough, but because of how it's being presented. **Most rejected CVs share the same handful of fixable mistakes.**

Here are the five most common ones, and what to do about each.

## 1. Sending a Generic CV to Every Job

This is the single most damaging thing you can do to your job search, and it's the most common. A generic CV is one that doesn't mirror the language of the job posting, doesn't emphasise the skills the employer cares most about, and reads like it could have been submitted to any of 50 different jobs.

The problem is structural: most hiring companies use Applicant Tracking Systems (ATS) that score your CV against the job description before a human ever reads it. A generic CV will score poorly even if you're actually a great fit. You get filtered out automatically.

**Fix it:** Tailor your CV for every application. Mirror the language in the job posting. Move relevant bullets to the top of each role. Update your summary for this specific position. Yes, it takes longer, but you'll need to send fewer applications to get the same number of interviews.

> One tailored application is worth five generic ones. Change how you think about the effort-to-result ratio.

## 2. Listing Duties Instead of Achievements

This is the second most common mistake. CV bullet points that describe job duties — what the role was supposed to involve — are nearly useless to a recruiter. Every person who held a similar role had those same duties. What makes you different is what you actually achieved.

Compare these two bullets for the same role:

- "Responsible for managing the social media accounts and creating content."
- "Grew Instagram following from 4,200 to 31,000 in 11 months through a weekly video series, driving a 40% increase in website traffic from social."

The second bullet is specific, quantified, and shows impact. It answers the recruiter's real question: "What would we get if we hired this person?"

**Fix it:** Go through every bullet point on your CV and ask "so what?" If the bullet doesn't answer that question with a result or impact, rewrite it. Add numbers wherever you can — percentages, revenue figures, team sizes, time saved, cost reduced. Estimates are fine; fabrications are not.

## 3. Burying the Skills an ATS Is Looking For

Most job seekers have a skills section that was assembled years ago and never touched since. It often has generic entries like "Microsoft Office" alongside actually relevant skills like "SQL" or "Salesforce", with no particular order or priority.

ATS systems scan your skills section (among other places) for the specific tools and competencies listed in the job description. If the job asks for "stakeholder management" and your CV says "relationship management", some systems won't make the connection. Exact match matters.

**Fix it:** For each job you apply for, cross-reference the skills mentioned in the job posting with your skills section. If you genuinely have a skill they're asking for, make sure it's listed in the exact same wording. Move the most relevant skills to the top of the section.

## 4. A Weak or Missing Summary

The CV summary (sometimes called a personal statement or professional profile) is the first thing a recruiter reads. It's a 2–4 sentence paragraph at the top of your CV that frames who you are and what kind of role you're looking for. And most people either don't have one, or have one so generic it communicates nothing.

A summary like "A motivated and results-driven professional with a passion for excellence" tells the recruiter nothing. Every candidate could write that sentence.

A strong summary is specific to the role, uses the language of the industry, and answers: "Why is this person interesting for this particular position?"

**Fix it:** Write your summary last, after you've reviewed the job posting. Include: your job title or area, your years of experience, two or three specific strengths that match this role, and a concrete result if you have one. Keep it to 3–4 lines. Rewrite it for each application.

## 5. Too Long, Too Dense, or Badly Formatted

Recruiters at larger companies may be reviewing 200+ CVs for a single role. They are not reading every word. Research consistently shows that the initial scan of a CV takes less than 10 seconds, and that scan is heavily influenced by how easy the document is to skim.

Common formatting problems:

- CVs longer than two pages when one would do
- Walls of text with no white space
- Tiny font size to cram in more content
- Inconsistent formatting (different fonts, bullet styles, date formats)
- Tables, columns, or graphics that ATS systems can't parse correctly

**Fix it:** Aim for one page if you have under 10 years of experience. Use a clean, single-column layout. Give sections clear headings and consistent spacing. If something doesn't make you look good for this specific role, cut it — irrelevant content makes strong content harder to find. Every line on your CV is competing for attention.

## The Underlying Pattern

All five of these mistakes share the same root cause: treating your CV as a record of your career rather than a targeted pitch for a specific job. Your CV isn't your autobiography, it's a sales document. Every word should be there because it helps you get this job.

That mental shift — from "here's what I've done" to "here's why you should hire me for this role" — is what separates a CV that gets ignored from one that gets you in the room.
