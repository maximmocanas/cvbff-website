How to Tailor Your CV to a Job Posting (Step-by-Step)

May 2026 · 7 min read

Here's something most job seekers figure out too late: sending the same CV to every job is one of the biggest reasons applications get rejected before a human ever reads them. ATS (Applicant Tracking Systems) score your CV against the job description automatically — and a generic CV will score poorly even if you're a perfect fit for the role.

Tailoring your CV doesn't mean lying or inflating your experience. It means presenting the genuine experience you have in a way that speaks directly to what this employer is looking for. Here's exactly how to do it.

Step 1: Read the Job Posting Like a Brief, Not an Ad

Before you touch your CV, spend 10 minutes studying the job description carefully. You're not reading it to decide if you want the job — you're reading it to extract the language and priorities of the hiring manager.

Look for:

  • Repeated words and phrases — if "stakeholder management" appears three times, it matters. If "cross-functional" appears once, it's probably standard filler.
  • The order of requirements — requirements listed first are almost always the most important. A job posting is written by a busy person who put the critical things at the top.
  • Specific tools or systems — "experience with Salesforce", "proficiency in SQL", "knowledge of GDPR compliance" — these are exact terms that ATS systems search for.
  • Soft skills with context — "ability to work in a fast-paced environment" is noise, but "managing relationships with C-suite executives" tells you something real about the role.

Step 2: Mirror the Language — Exactly

This is the most impactful single change you can make. If the job posting says "project management", your CV should say "project management" — not "project coordination", not "programme delivery". ATS systems are often doing exact keyword matching.

Don't paraphrase the job description. Mirror it. If they say "data-driven decision making", you say "data-driven decision making".

Go through your CV's summary, skills section, and role bullet points. For each one, ask: "Could this be rewritten using the language in the job posting?" If yes, rewrite it — provided the rewrite still accurately describes what you did.

Step 3: Lead with Relevance

Your CV summary (also called a personal statement or professional profile) is the first thing a recruiter reads — and it's your best chance to frame yourself for this specific role before they read anything else.

A generic summary might read: "Results-driven marketing professional with 5 years of experience across B2B and B2C environments."

A tailored summary for a content marketing role might read: "Content marketing manager with 5 years of experience driving organic traffic and pipeline growth for B2B SaaS companies. Experienced in SEO strategy, editorial planning, and cross-functional collaboration with sales and product teams."

The second version uses the language of the posting and signals immediately that this person understands the role.

Step 4: Reorder Your Bullet Points by Relevance

Within each role, you have multiple bullet points describing what you did. The recruiter will read the first two or three before their attention dips. Make sure those first bullets are the ones most relevant to this job posting.

If you're applying for a role that emphasises client relationships, lead with your client relationship wins — not your internal process improvements, even if you're prouder of those. The goal is to match what they need to what you've done, in the first few seconds.

Step 5: Quantify the Achievements That Matter Here

Numbers make claims credible and memorable. But not all numbers are equally relevant to every role. Tailor which metrics you lead with based on the job posting.

  • Applying for a sales role? Lead with revenue numbers and pipeline metrics.
  • Applying for a marketing role? Lead with traffic growth, conversion rates, and campaign ROI.
  • Applying for an operations role? Lead with efficiency gains, cost reductions, and process improvements.

You don't need to change the numbers — just the order and framing. "Managed a team of 8 and reduced delivery time by 30%" is more relevant for an operations role. "Generated £180K in new business from inbound campaigns" is more relevant for a growth role. Both might be true — pick the one that fits.

Step 6: Update Your Skills Section

Many job seekers treat their skills section as a static list they last updated years ago. It should actually be the easiest section to tailor quickly.

Look at the skills listed in the job posting. Do you have those skills? If yes and they're not on your CV, add them now (using the exact same terminology). If they're already on your CV but buried at the bottom, move them to the top. ATS systems often weight skills that appear earlier more heavily.

Step 7: Check Your CV Length

Once you've done all of the above, your CV is probably slightly longer than before — you've been adding things. Now cut ruthlessly. Every line on a CV is taking up space that could be used for something more relevant to this role.

A good test: for each bullet point, ask "does this help me get this specific job?" If the answer is no, cut it. Aim for a single, tight page. Recruiters scanning hundreds of CVs will read less than you expect.

How Long Does All This Take?

Done manually, properly, tailoring a CV for one role takes 20–45 minutes. That's the honest answer. Which is why most people don't do it — and why most people don't get as many interviews as they deserve.
